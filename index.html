Act I • The Dhaka Split-Screen

Why Does Cashless Work 30 Feet in the Air, But Collapse on the Asphalt Below?

01 · Viaduct

Up on the concrete viaduct of the Dhaka Metro Rail (DMTCL), commuters tap their DTCA Rapid Pass at enclosed turnstiles and board in seconds. The reader has power, the reader has signal, and the fare is verified before the doors close.

02 · Asphalt

Thirty feet below on Mirpur Road, a commuter climbs onto a crowded private bus with the exact same card and mobile wallet — and hits a wall. The same transaction now depends on a moving 4G signal and a conductor's willingness to wait.[1]

The Research Question (N = 150 Field Survey)

Group 8 surveyed 100 daily commuters and 50 transit workers to uncover why digital fare collection breaks down on Dhaka's buses.[7]

Act II • The 1.57 Satisfaction Cliff

"We Want to Pay Digitally—But Cashless Reaches Only 3 to 4 Routes."

01 · The myth

A common assumption is that Bangladeshi passengers prefer cash out of habit. Data from 100 Dhaka commuters proves the exact opposite: they rate digital payment highly on both ease and speed.

02 · The collapse

And yet satisfaction with the system they actually have collapses to 1.566 / 5.0, with a mode of 1.0 — "Strongly Disagree". The highest-frequency answer in the entire commuter dataset is the most negative one available.

Why the cliff exists

Cashless fare collection is live on only 3 to 4 routes. A commuter who wants to pay digitally simply cannot, most days — so the system is judged on its absence, not its interface.[3]

Act III • The Trip Chukti Trap (n = 50)

The "Trip Chukti" Trap: Why Every Digital Ticket Costs the Conductor Money.

01 · The split workforce

When Group 8 analyzed the 50 transit workers, the question on ease of daily digital collection produced the highest dispersion in the study (0.448) — the most divided answer anywhere in the dataset.

02 · Two jobs, one system

Stationary ticket counter clerks sit on fixed salaries and find digital payments manageable. Bus conductors operate under Dhaka's informal "Trip Chukti" daily cash lease — they must hand over a fixed cash target at the end of every shift.

The 1.85% MFS Drain:[2]

Every rupee that arrives digitally must be cashed out before it can pay for fuel or the owner's lease. The cash-out fee lands on the crew's own take-home pay, not on the operator.

Act IV • The Mutation of Fare Disputes

Cashless Didn't Stop Arguments on the Bus—It Just Changed What They Fight Over.

01 · The old fight

Workers moderately agree that cashless can reduce fare disputes (Mean = 3.5), but the correlation is weak (r = +0.142) — because the old argument did not disappear. It mutated.

02 · Two new fights

Kill the change argument and two replacements appear: a 4G dead zone under a flyover while thirty passengers push in behind, and a passenger flashing a forged bKash SMS that the conductor cannot verify from a moving bus.

Verified in the appendix

Both frictions appear as named barrier items on the transit worker questionnaire — "network instability and dropouts on moving buses" and "passengers showing forged confirmation messages (Fake SMS) during rush hours" (Appendix, p. 43).

                                Act V • The Regression Proof (Ch. 5.3)
                            

                            

                                "A Pretty App Cannot Fix a Broken Bus Network."
                            


                            

                                
01 · The test

                                

                                    Group 8 ran a multiple linear regression in Orange Data Mining (pp. 24–25) to ask a simple question: do the things people praise about cashless payment actually predict whether they are satisfied with it?
                                

                            


                            

                                
02 · The answer

                                

                                    No. Perceived ease of payment carries a coefficient of just +0.164, and reduced waiting time comes out negative (−0.085) — more speed, slightly less satisfaction. The study reports p > 0.05.
                                

                            


                            

                                
What the coefficients actually do

                                

                                    Move either slider and watch the predicted score. Even at a perfect 5.0 for both convenience measures, the model cannot promise a satisfied passenger — because the thing that breaks the system is not in the model at all.
                                

                                

                                    Source: Ch. 5.3, pp. 24–25. The p > 0.05 statement appears in the Executive Summary, p. 4; the study publishes no per-predictor p-values and no R².[5]

                                Act VI • The Chapter 8 Blueprint
                            

                            

                                How to Turn a 1.57 Failure into a Working Cashless Megacity.
                            


                            

                                
01 · The conclusion

                                

                                    The report's conclusion is unmistakable: digitalizing Dhaka's commute requires structural policy reform, not just a prettier app. Technological ease is currently being entirely cancelled out by systemic unreliability.
                                

                            


                            

                                
Policy Blueprint (p. 32):

                                

                                    Toggle the four structural reforms to see how eliminating cash-out fees and deploying offline NFC terminals transforms the street reality. Each enacted reform emits an NFC verification pulse — the 0.2-second offline tap the report recommends.
                                

                            


                            

                                
02 · The ceiling

                                

                                    All four together project a system satisfaction of 4.80 / 5.0 — from a reported baseline of 1.57. None of these reforms makes the payment app better. Every one of them removes a structural obstruction.

                Sources, Notes & Method
            

            
What Is Reported, What Is Modelled, and What the Study Does Not Say

            

                Every figure in this documentary is either reproduced from the source study or explicitly marked as a modelled illustration. Nothing else is presented as a finding.
            


            
    
                
  1. [1]Elevation and tap timings are editorial illustration. The study describes the metro as an enclosed, controlled system in contrast to street-level bus operations (Ch. 3.5, p. 16) but publishes no height measurement and no hardware timing. The "+30 ft", "0.28s" and "14.2s" figures are dramatisations.
    2. 
                
  2. [2]The 1.85% cash-out fee is an external rate, not a study finding. It is the prevailing bKash agent cash-out rate at the time of writing. The study documents that MFS cash-out fees erode transit worker earnings (Ch. 6.4, p. 28) and recommends zero-fee merchant accounts (Ch. 8, p. 32) without publishing a percentage.
    3. 
                
  3. [3]Route coverage is stated as "3 to 4 specific routes" (Ch. 6.1, p. 27; Ch. 8, p. 32). The study publishes no total route count for Dhaka, so no coverage percentage is shown anywhere in this piece.
    4. 
                
  4. [4]The Trip Chukti ledger is a model. Fixed daily costs of ৳5,500 fuel and ৳2,000 owner lease are illustrative assumptions, and the 1.85% fee is applied to gross digital collections. The study reports the trip chukti system qualitatively and publishes no rupee figures.
    5. 
                
  5. [5]Regression coefficients are exact; p-values are coarse. Intercept 1.27795, Q6 +0.163641, Q7 −0.084696 (Ch. 5.3, pp. 24–25). The study states p > 0.05 (Executive Summary, p. 4) but publishes no per-predictor p-values and no R², so none are shown.
    6. 
                
  6. [6]The Act VI projection is invented for this piece and is labelled as such wherever it appears. It applies fixed additive weights to the reported baseline of 1.57 and reaches 4.80 only when all four reforms are enacted.
    7. 
                
  7. [7]Sample composition. n = 150: 100 daily commuters (MRT, BRTC and private bus users) and 50 transit workers — bus drivers, conductors and ticket counter staff. Non-probability convenience sampling across the Dhaka Metropolitan Area; two structured questionnaires on a 5-point Likert scale; analysis in Orange Data Mining (Ch. 4.1–4.5, pp. 19–20).
    8. 
            


            

                
What the study does not contain

                

                    The report publishes no respondent demographics, no frequency or percentage tables for any questionnaire item, no per-predictor significance values, no R², and no route names or coverage map. The appendix contains the two blank questionnaires only. This documentary therefore shows none of those things rather than estimating them.
